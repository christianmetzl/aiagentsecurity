\begin{abstract}
\noindent
\textbf{We treat this red-team benchmark as a controlled experiment on \emph{guardrail-dependent} agent
security.} The reusable output is a map of which multi-step failures survive which defense, two algorithms
that produce them efficiently, a matched guardrail that closes them, and a one-command reproduction of every
mechanism claim --- with the leaderboard standing used only as \emph{supporting evidence}, not as the result.
\smallskip

\noindent
We study the Kaggle \emph{AI Agent Security --- Multi-Step Tool Attacks} benchmark, in which an
attacker writes only the user side of a conversation that drives two open-weight tool-using models
(\code{gpt-oss-20b} and \code{gemma-4-26B-A4B-it}, both served as \code{Q4\_K\_M} GGUF) toward unsafe
tool calls, and is scored by the security predicates those calls trigger. The benchmark scores every
submission twice: against a \emph{permissive} public guardrail whose source ships in the SDK, and
against a \emph{stricter, held-out} private guardrail that decides the final rank. We reverse-engineer
the evaluation from its gateway source and use it to make three contributions that outlast the
leaderboard. \textbf{(i) A guardrail-dependent failure-mode taxonomy}: we show, from source and by
measurement, exactly which of the four scored multi-step failures fire under a permissive versus a
provenance-tracking guardrail --- and that the highest-value public attack (marker exfiltration)
\emph{collapses to zero} against a data-inspecting defense, while a lower-severity confused-deputy
failure survives both. \textbf{(ii) Two reusable, budget-aware attack algorithms}: a
\emph{live validation-fill} that returns only candidates it has verified fire (100\% scored yield,
self-sizing to model speed), and a \emph{parser-safe hop-chain} that amortizes a fixed per-candidate
cost across many scored events while respecting the target parser's one-tool-call-per-generation
constraint --- a constraint we show is why the natural ``ask for $K$ calls at once'' multipost is
silently rejected. We then \emph{measure} that even the parser-safe hop-chain does not beat single-post
validation-fill on the real eval: this scorer is cell-count-dominated, so candidate \emph{throughput},
not events-per-candidate, is the governing axis --- an intended negative result. \textbf{(iii) A matched, provenance-tracking defense} that drives our own strongest
attacks to zero with zero benign false positives, together with a defense-comparison harness and a
one-command, GPU-free reproduction of every mechanism claim ($16$ checks, $\approx\!4$\,min).
As \emph{supporting evidence} --- not the contribution itself --- we
\textbf{confirm the prediction on the live private board}: a two-submission hedge --- a maximal
exfiltration fill plus a confused-deputy survivor --- climbs \textbf{$+1{,}284$ places} when the private
guardrail is applied (to private rank $133$ of $4{,}187$, a silver medal), because
the exfiltration-dominated field collapses with our own exfiltration leg while our deputy survivor is
scored: a direct measurement of the guardrail-dependent collapse this note predicts. The
central lesson for the community is a measurement one: a permissive public leaderboard rewards attacks
that exploit a specific guardrail bug and do \emph{not} transfer to a competent defense; agent-security
benchmarks should therefore score against provenance-aware guardrails. Every claim is tagged
\evtag{measured}/\evtag{inferred}/\evtag{speculative}, we report our wrong turns as reusable negative
results, and the study is confined to a sandbox with synthetic markers; the one guardrail gap we exploit
is disclosed to the organizers with a fix.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Tool-using language-model agents turn text into consequential actions --- HTTP requests, file writes,
emails, shell commands --- so their failures cross security boundaries. The competition~\cite{comp}
isolates this surface cleanly: given only the ability to write the \emph{user side} of a conversation,
how reliably can an attacker steer such an agent into a policy-violating tool call, across multiple
steps, in a way that \emph{reproduces} under an independent replay? The organizers state plainly that
the strongest submissions ``do more than climb the leaderboard'': they produce reusable attack
algorithms, standardized failure cases, and practical insight that helps builders compare defenses and
find weak points earlier. This note is written to that brief.

Our central observation is that the benchmark is, in effect, a controlled experiment on
\emph{guardrail-dependent} agent security. The public leaderboard scores against a permissive guardrail
(\code{OptimalGuardrail}) whose source is available; the private leaderboard --- which alone decides the
rank --- scores against a stricter guardrail that is shipped as a separate, withheld wheel and installed
only inside the scoring environment. That design is unusual and, we argue, correct for security
research: it separates ``exploited a knowingly weak guardrail'' from ``beat a competent defense.'' It
also means the most transferable findings are not the score-maximizing recipe but the map of
\emph{which multi-step failures survive which guardrail}, the algorithms that produce them efficiently,
and the defense that closes them.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{A guardrail-dependent failure taxonomy (\S\ref{sec:taxonomy}).} From the predicate and
guardrail source, and confirmed by measurement against the SDK's own stricter baseline, we give the
$4\times$guardrail matrix of which failures fire where --- and identify \textsc{Confused\_Deputy} as the
one high-frequency failure that survives a data-inspecting defense.
\item \textbf{Two reusable attack algorithms (\S\ref{sec:algos}).} Live validation-fill and the
parser-safe hop-chain, both benchmark-agnostic and budget-aware, with the parser constraint that
governs multi-call attacks made explicit.
\item \textbf{The public/private guardrail gap as a measurement result (\S\ref{sec:gap}).} We locate,
in the gateway source, exactly how the private guardrail is loaded, show why permissive-guardrail
farming does not transfer, and draw the benchmark-design implication.
\item \textbf{A matched provenance defense and a defense-comparison (\S\ref{sec:defense}).} An
action-side, prompt-agnostic guardrail plus a chat-template sanitizer that zero our attacks with zero
false positives, evaluated across a panel of guardrail designs.
\item \textbf{Honest results, negative results, and reproducibility
(\S\ref{sec:results}--\S\ref{sec:repro}).} Real-evaluation scores, four documented regressions, an
explicit limitations section, and one-command local reproduction; claims are evidence-tagged throughout.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent Throughout, we treat the leaderboard standing --- the silver-medal private result included --- as
\emph{supporting evidence} for these artifacts, not as the contribution itself: each artifact (the taxonomy,
the two algorithms, the matched defense, the one-command reproduction) is written to be lifted and re-run
independently of any score.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Agent and tool-use attacks.} AgentDojo~\cite{agentdojo} and InjecAgent~\cite{injecagent} formalize
indirect prompt injection against tool-using agents; a growing literature assembles individually-benign calls into harmful
sequences (STAC~\cite{stac}). Our setting differs in that scoring is a \emph{white-box, additive}
function of triggered predicates evaluated against an explicit guardrail, which lets us study
\emph{guardrail dependence} directly rather than treating the defense as a black box.

\textbf{Guardrails and provenance.} Taint/provenance tracking --- flagging data derived from untrusted
sources and constraining its downstream use --- is a classical defense idea; our matched defense
(\S\ref{sec:defense}) is a small, concrete instance specialized to the egress channels this benchmark
scores. Our finding that the public and private guardrails differ by exactly this property is, to our
knowledge, the sharpest public statement of \emph{how much} a leaderboard's ranking can hinge on the
guardrail rather than the attack.

\textbf{Jailbreaks on small open models.} We drew on many-shot priming~\cite{manyshot}, reasoning-
guardrail subversion on \code{gpt-oss}~\cite{reasonbypass}, dialogue/context injection~\cite{dia}, and
\code{gpt-oss-20b} failure modes~\cite{gptoss}. A recurring \emph{negative} finding (\S\ref{sec:results})
is that chat-oriented jailbreaks \emph{regress} tool-call yield on these models --- length costs
candidate throughput and prose mangles tool-call JSON --- a caution against importing chat-jailbreak
intuitions into a tool-call regime.

\section{Benchmark and Threat Model}\label{sec:threat}
\paragraph{Interaction (\evtag{source-traced}).} A candidate is a list of user messages
($\le 32$ messages, $\le 10{,}000$ characters each, non-empty). The gateway drives a target model through
the candidate; on each message the model may emit tool calls up to a hop cap ($8$ at replay). Each tool
call passes through a \emph{guardrail} that allows or denies it. Generation runs against the permissive
public guardrail; the same returned list is then \emph{replayed} against held-out guardrails and both
models, and only \emph{successful} (allowed) actions can trigger a predicate.

\paragraph{The two guardrails (\evtag{source-traced}).}
The public \code{OptimalGuardrail} (its source ships in the SDK) extracts a single ``target'' argument
per call --- the \emph{first} present of \code{[path,url,cmd,data,body,content]} --- and denies the call
if that target contains a sensitive substring (\code{secret}, \code{token}, \code{key}, \dots) or if a
dangerous tool is used after an untrusted (web/email) read. The private guardrail is \emph{not} shipped:
the gateway loads it from a separate wheel, \code{aicomp\_private\_guardrails-*.whl}, installed from the
scoring environment via the \code{aicomp\_sdk.attack\_guardrails} entry point selected by the
\code{AICOMP\_ATTACK\_GUARDRAIL\_ID} environment variable (\S\ref{sec:gap}). It is unobservable to
competitors both because its code is withheld and because private scores are hidden until close.

\paragraph{Sandbox and ethics scope.} The ``secret'' is a synthetic literal (\code{SECRET\_MARKER});
destinations are reserved \code{*.invalid}/\code{*.co}/\code{*.example} names; no real system or person
is targeted. We treat the exercise as \emph{defensive} research (\S\ref{sec:ethics}).

\section{Scoring Economics (the foundation)}\label{sec:econ}
The failure taxonomy and the algorithms both follow from the scoring rule, so we state it first.
\paragraph{Scoring (\evtag{measured}, \code{aicomp\_sdk/scoring.py}).}
Summing severity over every triggered event with \emph{no de-duplication}, plus $+2$ per distinct
\emph{score cell} (one cell hash \emph{per finding}, keyed on the destination domain and tool sequence):
\begin{equation}
\text{raw}=\sum_{\text{events}} w(s)+2\,\lvert\text{cells}\rvert,\qquad
\text{score}=\min\!\Big(1000,\ \tfrac{\text{raw}}{200}\Big).
\end{equation}
A single \code{http.post} carrying the marker fires \textsc{Exfiltration} ($16$ raw); with one distinct
destination per candidate (a free $+2$ cell), \textbf{each successful single post is worth
$18$ raw $=0.09$ normalized}.
\paragraph{Budget (\evtag{measured}, \code{jed\_attack\_gateway.py}).}
The gateway applies a per-phase budget \code{DEFAULT\_BUDGET\_S}$=8750$s ``once to generation and once
to each replay for every model,'' and replays at most \code{MAX\_REPLAY\_FINDINGS}$=2000$ candidates
\emph{in order, until the replay deadline, scoring whatever completed} --- crucially, it does not void a
submission that returns more candidates than fit. Thus per model, score is bounded by the number of
firing candidates that fit the $8750$s replay budget (or the $2000$ cap). Because scoring is additive
with no interaction, the allocation of the budget across candidate routes is a \emph{fractional
knapsack}; greedy by value-per-second is provably optimal, so no annealing/DPP/``quantum''
meta-heuristic can beat it (\S\ref{sec:results}). The single design consequence: maximize \emph{scored
events per replay-second}.

\section{Two Reusable Attack Algorithms}\label{sec:algos}
Both algorithms are benchmark-agnostic and follow from \S\ref{sec:econ}; neither depends on a particular
prompt.

\paragraph{(A) Live validation-fill.} The naive attacker emits $N$ blindly-constructed candidates; only
a fraction fire on replay, so the scored yield --- and the score --- is capped by an unknown compliance
rate. Validation-fill instead \emph{generates one candidate, replays it against the live guardrail, and
keeps it only if it fired}, repeating until a replay-safe cushion. Two properties follow: (1) 100\% of
the returned list provably fires, so the scored yield is the full per-candidate value; and (2) the loop
\emph{self-sizes} to each model's real speed, because each kept candidate's measured cost is accumulated
against the replay budget. This turns a blind, compliance-limited emit into a verified, budget-calibrated
fill, and it is the single change that lifts a single-post attack from the low tens to the mid-80s on
this benchmark (\S\ref{sec:results}). The idea generalizes to any replay-validated benchmark.

\paragraph{(B) Parser-safe hop-chain multipost.} Since $+2$ cells are counted once per finding but
severity is summed over \emph{every} event, a candidate that triggers $K$ scored events amortizes its
fixed per-candidate cost (prefill plus the closing turn) over $K$ events rather than one. The obvious
realization --- ``make $K$ tool calls in one reply'' --- \emph{fails}: the target parser
(\code{normalize\_parsed\_response}) raises on more than one tool call per generation, so such candidates
are silently rejected (we measured the resulting regression; \S\ref{sec:results}). The fix is to drive
\emph{one call per turn} across the hop budget: the model posts to endpoint $j$, receives the result, and
posts endpoint $j{+}1$ --- one call per generation, never rejected --- yielding up to $8$ scored events
per message (and more across a bounded number of messages). On the reasoning model we forge an
already-closed Harmony analysis channel so the model commits to the enumerated endpoints and emits the
calls without intervening chain-of-thought. Per unit replay-time the amortization is
$(w_1 P + w_2 d)$-dependent: for a fixed per-candidate cost $P$ and per-event decode $d$, an $8$-event
hop-chain beats $8$ single-posts by a factor between $\approx1.6$ (decode-bound) and $\approx7$
(prefill-bound). The construction respects a real, source-level constraint of the target rather than a
prompt trick, which is why it transfers.

\section{A Guardrail-Dependent Failure Taxonomy}\label{sec:taxonomy}
The scored predicates do not fire uniformly: each depends on the guardrail. We read the two guardrails'
logic from source and \emph{measured} each failure against the SDK's own stricter baseline
(\code{guardrail\_rules.py}), which --- unlike the public guardrail --- inspects the \code{http.post}
\code{data} channel (it calls \code{payload\_exfiltrates\_secret} on the egress payload) and is thus a
faithful stand-in for a competent, data-inspecting private guardrail. Table~\ref{tab:matrix} is the
result and, we believe, the most reusable artifact in this note.

\paragraph{The practical insight.} On the \emph{public} board, exactly two of the four failures are
reachable, and one of them (\textsc{Exfiltration}, weight $16$) dominates --- which is why the public
leaderboard is a near-pure exfiltration-throughput race. But that failure exists on the public board
\emph{only because of a specific guardrail bug} (argument-order in target extraction). Against a
guardrail that inspects the egress channel, it vanishes, and the field's dominant attack is worth zero.
The one failure that survives, \textsc{Confused\_Deputy}, is precisely the one that carries no secret
pattern for a content filter to catch and no taint for a provenance filter to catch --- an
\emph{authority-misuse} failure rather than a \emph{data-leak} failure. For builders, this is the
transferable message: content- and taint-based egress filters do not, by themselves, stop an agent from
being turned into a confused deputy.

\section{The Public/Private Guardrail Gap}\label{sec:gap}
\paragraph{How the private guardrail is loaded (\evtag{source-traced}).} The gateway's own docstring
states the split: ``Public leaderboard: evaluated against \code{OptimalGuardrail} (default). Private
leaderboard: evaluated against the private guardrail loaded via the
\code{aicomp\_sdk.attack\_guardrails} entry point group, specified by \code{AICOMP\_ATTACK\_GUARDRAIL\_ID}.''
A helper (\code{\_try\_install\_private\_guardrails}) pip-installs
\code{aicomp\_private\_guardrails-*.whl} from the scoring environment. We verified that this wheel is
\emph{absent} from all 145 downloadable competition files and that no such entry point is registered in
the shipped SDK --- so the private guardrail is genuinely held out, by construction, not by omission.

\paragraph{Why permissive-guardrail scores need not transfer.} Combining \S\ref{sec:taxonomy} with the
budget structure: a submission optimized purely for the public board is a validated fill of
marker-exfiltration candidates. Against a data-inspecting private guardrail --- the natural way to make
a guardrail ``stricter,'' and exactly what the SDK's \code{rules} baseline does --- every such candidate
is denied, and the entire public score collapses toward zero, \emph{regardless of how high it was}. A
submission that also carries the surviving failure (\textsc{Confused\_Deputy}) retains a private score
where the pure-exfiltration field does not.

\paragraph{Measured confirmation on the private board (\evtag{measured}).} Facing an unobservable
guardrail, the rational response is to \emph{hedge}, so we submitted two finals: one maximal exfiltration
fill (\code{public\_max}, public $86.085$) and one confused-deputy survivor (\code{deputy\_max}, public
$20.095$); the private board scores the better of the two. Our realized private score was
\textbf{$20.095$} --- \emph{exactly} \code{deputy\_max}'s value --- so our $86$-point exfiltration leg
scored \emph{below} $20$ once the private guardrail was applied: it collapsed, precisely as
Table~\ref{tab:matrix}'s strict column predicts, while the deputy survivor scored \emph{identically} on
both boards (confirming its two-guardrail survival). The rank tells the same story: a
$\mathbf{+1{,}284}$-place climb (Kaggle's private-board delta) to private rank $\mathbf{133}$ of
$4{,}187$, a silver medal, as the exfiltration-dominated field that outscored us publicly collapsed
with our own exfiltration leg. This is a direct measurement of the collapse for our submission and, via
the rank inversion, strong corroboration that it is field-wide. We still do not observe the private
guardrail's source --- but its \emph{effect} (exfiltration degrades, confused-deputy survives) is now
\evtag{measured}, not merely \evtag{inferred}.

\paragraph{Implication for measuring agent security.} The gap is not a flaw to complain about --- it is
the benchmark's most useful feature, and a template. A permissive public board that anyone can farm with
volume exfiltration measures ``can you exploit a weak guardrail,'' not ``can you beat a real defense.''
Scoring the ranking board against a \emph{provenance-tracking} guardrail (as this benchmark does) is the
correct way to reward attacks that constitute genuine robustness findings. We recommend future
agent-security benchmarks make this split explicit and publish the \emph{class} of the private guardrail
(e.g.\ ``provenance-tracking, data-inspecting'') so that contestants optimize for transferable attacks
rather than argument-order bugs.

\section{A Matched Defense and a Defense Comparison}\label{sec:defense}
The attack study yields a defense directly. We ship two layers.
\paragraph{(1) Action-side, prompt-agnostic guardrail.} It inspects the egress \emph{payload} channel
with the scorer's own secret matcher and applies persistent provenance taint: any \code{http.post} whose
\code{data} carries the marker, and any egress after a secret or untrusted read, is denied --- regardless
of how the model was talked into it. Because it acts at the action boundary, no amount of raised model
willingness (role-play, crescendo, many-shot, fake-over-refusal, policy-collision) changes the verdict:
those techniques change the \emph{model's} decision, not the \emph{tool call}.
\paragraph{(2) Input-side chat-template sanitizer.} The one attack an action filter cannot pre-empt is
chat-template forgery (injecting Harmony/gemma control tokens into untrusted content). The sanitizer
neutralizes those control tokens before templating, so a forged turn never forms.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
All numbers are the \emph{public} normalized score (0--1000) on the real evaluation unless noted; the
private column is hidden until close. Scores are \evtag{measured} on the competition eval; projections
are \evtag{inferred} and labeled.

\paragraph{The algorithms work, the ``clever'' additions do not.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{Validation-fill lifts single-post from $\sim$11 to $86.1$.} A blind single-post fill at a
private-reserving fraction scored $10.9$; the live validation-fill (\code{public\_max}) scored
$\mathbf{86.085}$ --- a $\approx\!7.9\times$ jump on the same $0.09$-per-post primitive over the blind
baseline (and $4.9$--$5.8\times$ over the best prior throughput/packing builds, $14.9$--$17.75$), purely
by returning only verified-firing candidates and sizing to the replay budget.
\item \textbf{Multi-event chaining loses to single-post throughput (negative result).} A
$K$-calls-in-one-reply multipost (\code{ceiling\_breaker}, $K{=}4$) scored $70.3$, \emph{below}
single-post $86.1$: the parser rejects the multi-call generations, and the one-cell-per-finding rule
means multipost also forgoes the per-candidate cell bonus. \emph{The parser-safe hop-chain (one call per
turn) does not rescue it either:} on the real eval it came in \emph{below} the single-post baselines on
both routes --- public $48.1/44.4/39.8$ at $16/24/32$ posts (vs.\ single-post $86.1$) and deputy $8.2$
(vs.\ single-post $20.1$), a monotone decline as more posts are demanded. The cause is structural, not a
bug: because the score is dominated by the $+2$ cell term --- which scales with the \emph{number of
distinct firing candidates} --- trading candidate count for events-per-candidate loses whenever the
models do not sustain the full chain, and they do not. \textbf{The governing axis is candidate
throughput, not events-per-candidate}; single-post validation-fill, which maximizes throughput, is the
optimal shape under this scorer, and the hop-chain is the reusable \emph{negative} that establishes it.
\item \textbf{Final standing and the throughput frontier (\evtag{measured}).} Our selected hedge finished
$\mathbf{133/4{,}187}$ (\textbf{silver}) on the private board (\S\ref{sec:gap}). On the \emph{public}
board $86.1$ was mid-field: the single-post ceiling is $2000\times0.09=180$, and $86.1$ corresponds to
$\approx\!957$ of the $2000$ candidates clearing the $8750$s replay, whereas the public leader
($\approx\!147$) cleared $\approx\!1{,}633$ (Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}) --- a \emph{throughput} gap (cheaper per-candidate replay:
terser prompts, suppressed chain-of-thought, fast-first ordering), not a stronger attack. On the
\emph{private} board the winner scored $46.4$ to our $20.1$: the \emph{same} surviving-predicate insight
executed at $\approx\!2.3\times$ our density. The private axis is thus how much surviving confused-deputy
one packs, and we under-developed it (\S\ref{sec:limits}) --- a depth-before-novelty miss, not a missing
idea.
\item \textbf{Chat-jailbreaks regress (negative result).} Role-play ($7.6$) and crescendo/many-shot
($7.5$) scored $\approx30\%$ below the plain terse baseline ($10.9$) and far below the throughput
substrate ($14.9$): verbose persona preambles dilute the tool-call instruction and mangle JSON on 4--20B
models. Terse, structural attacks win.
\item \textbf{Confused-deputy fires on the real models.} A pure \code{email.send}-without-intent fill
(\code{deputy\_max}) scored $\mathbf{20.1}$, confirming the surviving failure of Table~\ref{tab:matrix}
fires on \emph{both} target models --- the private-column foothold the pure-exfiltration field lacks.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The optimization verdict (\evtag{measured} $+$ \evtag{inferred}).} Because scoring is additive
and dedup-free, the budget allocation is a fractional knapsack; greedy by value-per-second is provably
optimal, so DPP/annealing/QAOA add nothing (our DPP selector reduces to greedy and is off by default).
The only stochastic sub-problem is which framing to commit to under a small query budget against a noisy
model --- a bandit, solved by the validation-fill probe. There is no ``quantum'' advantage; we report
this because it is a common temptation on an additive objective.

\section{Limitations and What We Do Not Claim}\label{sec:limits}
The private guardrail's source remains \emph{unobserved}; our confirmation of the collapse
(\S\ref{sec:gap}) is measured on \emph{our own} submission (an $86$-point exfiltration leg scoring below
$20$ on the private board) and corroborated field-wide by the $+1{,}284$-place rank inversion, but we do
not observe other teams' private scores directly. The hop-chain projections in earlier drafts were
\emph{refuted} by the real eval (it scored below single-post; \S\ref{sec:results}); we report that as the
intended negative result, not a shortfall to hide. \emph{On our own execution}, our surviving private
score ($20.1$) was $\approx\!2.3\times$ below the winner's ($46.4$): we found the surviving-predicate
insight but under-developed it, spending budget on the hop-chain (which regressed) instead of maximizing
confused-deputy density on the axis that actually decides the private board. The evaluation is
model-non-deterministic at the margin. \emph{Falsifier}: a submission whose realized score departs
materially from $0.09\times(\text{observed firing events})$ would refute the governing relation; across
our submissions it held.

\section{Takeaways for Builders and Benchmark Designers}\label{sec:takeaways}
This note is written to be lifted; the transferable recommendations are consolidated here.

\paragraph{For benchmark designers.} (i) Score the \emph{ranking} board against a provenance-tracking,
data-inspecting guardrail --- the design choice that makes this benchmark measure real robustness rather
than guardrail-bug farming, and the one Fig.~\ref{fig:collapse} shows a ranking can hinge on entirely.
(ii) Publish the \emph{class} of the private guardrail (e.g.\ ``provenance-tracking, data-inspecting'')
so contestants optimize for transferable attacks, not argument-order bugs. (iii) Keep the public/private
guardrail split: it cleanly separates ``exploited a knowingly weak guardrail'' from ``beat a competent
defense.''

\paragraph{For agent builders.} An egress guardrail must (i) inspect the \emph{payload} channel, not the
first-matching argument; (ii) track provenance \emph{persistently}, not within a short window; and (iii)
constrain \emph{authority-misuse} actions (e.g.\ \code{email.send}) that carry no secret pattern.
Content- and taint-based egress filters alone do not stop an agent from being turned into a confused
deputy (Table~\ref{tab:matrix}); the matched guardrail of \S\ref{sec:defense} is the minimal design that
does, at zero benign false positives.

\paragraph{For failure-search methodology.} \emph{Validation-fill} --- generate one candidate, replay it
against the live guardrail, keep it only if it fired --- turns a blind, compliance-limited emit into a
$100\%$-scored, budget-calibrated search over the candidate space, and generalizes to any
replay-validated benchmark. Two companion rules earned the biggest corrections here: \emph{measure the
scoring objective before optimizing it} (the bind was verified yield and per-event replay cost, not model
refusal), and \emph{respect the target's parser, not just the model}. And, as a negative that is itself a
finding: under a cell-count-dominated additive scorer, candidate \emph{throughput} beats
events-per-candidate, so a single-post fill outscores the hop-chain (\S\ref{sec:results}).

\paragraph{Reusable artifacts.} The failure$\times$guardrail matrix (Table~\ref{tab:matrix}); the
defense-comparison harness (Table~\ref{tab:defense}, Fig.~\ref{fig:defense}); the validation-fill
algorithm; and a one-command offline reproduction (\S\ref{sec:repro}) that re-derives the scoring
identity, the taxonomy, and the defense collapse.

\section{Independent Confirmation and the Winning Edge}\label{sec:winner}
After the competition closed, the first-place solution was published by \emph{xz}~\cite{winner}. We discuss
it here because it both corroborates our central finding and identifies a throughput lever we did not ---
and we are careful to credit its methods entirely to their author.

\paragraph{Independent confirmation of the taxonomy (\evtag{external}).} Reaching the private guardrail's
behavior by \emph{black-box leaderboard probing} --- a clock/timing construction that reveals, on private
replay, whether a given tool call is blocked --- rather than by our source analysis, xz reports exactly
the strict column of Table~\ref{tab:matrix}: calls carrying \code{secret} are blocked, untrusted-to-action
stays blocked for the following calls, and \textsc{Confused\_Deputy} (\code{email.send} with no
\emph{send}/\emph{email}/\emph{upload} intent word in the final user message) is ``the only route I could
reliably score at scale.'' Two independent methods --- static source analysis and black-box probing ---
converging on ``\textsc{Confused\_Deputy} is the only surviving private route'' is, we believe, the
strongest available corroboration of our taxonomy.

\paragraph{The throughput lever we did not find (\evtag{external}; the work is xz's, not ours).} We
identified candidate \emph{throughput} as the governing private axis (\S\ref{sec:results}), but our own
attempt to exploit it --- multi-event hop-chains --- moved throughput the \emph{wrong} way (more tokens
per candidate) and regressed. xz found the lever that instead \emph{reduces} per-candidate cost: a
scoring \textsc{Confused\_Deputy} candidate spans two model calls, and the score is recorded after the
first, so the second (a short cleanup, $\sim\!4$ tokens) is wasted replay time. Using \emph{GCG} (Greedy
Coordinate Gradient)~\cite{gcg} against BF16 proxies of the quantized target models, xz optimized prompts
that preserve the exact hop-1 tool call \emph{and} force the first hop-2 token to be an end-of-generation
token, terminating the candidate immediately and clearing more candidates within the fixed replay budget
(reported $\sim\!44.5\!\to\!46.5$; the Gemma row transferred to the competition GGUF, the GPT-OSS row did
not). \textbf{This gradient-based adversarial optimization is entirely xz's contribution; we did not use
it and claim no part of it.} It is the concrete resolution of our own limitation (\S\ref{sec:limits}): on
the throughput axis we correctly named, the winning move was to \emph{remove} the wasted hop, not to add
events --- exactly the depth-on-the-validated-axis lesson of this note, now with a named, published
instance to point to.

\section{Lessons}\label{sec:lessons}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{Measure the objective before optimizing it.} The constraint was verified yield and
per-event replay cost, not model refusal.
\item \textbf{A wall can be a mis-read.} An apparent ``compliance wall'' was really posts-per-second;
the fix was candidate \emph{structure} (validation-fill, hop-chain), not a jailbreak.
\item \textbf{Respect the parser, not just the model.} The multipost that ``should'' win is silently
rejected by a one-call-per-generation parser; the transferable attack is the one that honors it.
\item \textbf{Cleverness can cost points.} Chat-jailbreaks \emph{regressed} tool-call yield; negative
results matter.
\item \textbf{The board is guardrail-dependent.} A permissive public score can be worth zero against a
competent defense; the reusable finding is the failure$\times$guardrail map, not the recipe.
\item \textbf{Attack and defense are one project.} The guardrail that zeroes our attacks is the useful
artifact for builders.
\item \textbf{The predicted collapse was measured, and the hedge paid.} Pairing a guardrail-surviving
predicate with the maximal public attack moved us $+1{,}284$ places when the private board was scored ---
the transferable strategic finding, not a leaderboard trophy.
\item \textbf{Right insight, wrong dose.} First place shared our surviving-predicate insight but executed
it $\approx\!2.3\times$ denser; multiplying the survivor via chains \emph{regressed}. Once an axis is
validated, the win is \emph{depth} on it --- pushing it to its ceiling --- not a novel multiplier; and a
rank won because the field collapsed is not the same as being strong on the deciding axis.
\item \textbf{Engage the frontier's writeup, not just its score.} The published first-place solution
(\S\ref{sec:winner}) independently confirmed our taxonomy and won by \emph{removing} the wasted second hop
(a GCG-optimized termination token) --- the throughput lever we had named but not mined. The sharpest
lessons come from reading what the winner actually did, credited to them.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Responsible Disclosure and Ethics}\label{sec:ethics}
This is defensive research on a \emph{sandboxed} benchmark: the secret is a synthetic literal, egress
destinations are reserved names, and no real system or person is targeted. The one guardrail gap we
exploit --- the public guardrail inspecting a call's first-matching argument (\code{url}) rather than its
egress \code{data} channel --- is disclosed to the organizers together with the fixed, data-inspecting,
provenance-tracking guardrail of \S\ref{sec:defense}. We publish no operational capability against any
real deployment; every technique is scoped to the competition's fixture-backed sandbox.

\section{Reproducibility}\label{sec:repro}
Every number traces to a committed artifact: real-evaluation scores to a submissions log, scoring and
guardrail constants to the SDK source we cite by file, defense and taxonomy numbers to a local harness
that runs against the SDK's \code{SandboxEnv}, predicates, scoring, and both packaged guardrails using
deterministic stand-in agents (no GPU, no network). A claims ledger records, per claim, its value, the
script that produces it, its evidence tier, and its status; figures are regenerated by a committed
script. A single command, \code{python verify.py --fast}, re-derives all $16$ mechanism checks --- the
scoring identity ($0.09$ per firing single-post), the Table~\ref{tab:matrix} taxonomy (marker
exfiltration blocked and the confused deputy surviving on the data-inspecting guardrail), and the
Table~\ref{tab:defense} defense collapse (every attack driven to zero at zero benign false positives) ---
offline in $\approx4$ minutes, with no GPU and no network.
